About Colorblindness

Understanding Color Vision Deficiency

Colorblindness affects roughly 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women globally. It doesn't mean seeing in black and white — it means certain colors appear similar or indistinguishable from each other.

Types of Color Vision Deficiency

Protanopia

Red-Blind

Missing red cone cells. Reds appear very dark. Common — affects ~1% of males. Often confused with deuteranopia.

Deuteranopia

Green-Blind

Missing green cone cells. Most common form. Green and red appear similar. Affects ~1% of males.

Tritanopia

Blue-Blind

Missing blue cone cells. Rare condition. Blue and green appear similar; red and pink look alike.

Achromatopsia

Monochromacy

Complete inability to perceive color. Extremely rare. Usually accompanied by light sensitivity (photophobia).

The Ishihara Method

Developed by Dr. Shinobu Ishihara in 1917, the test uses pseudoisochromatic plates — circular images made of dots in varying colors and sizes. Hidden within the patterns are numbers that people with color vision deficiencies struggle to identify, because the number and background colors appear the same to them. Our digital version renders authentic dot patterns programmatically across 24 carefully designed plates.
